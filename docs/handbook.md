# The Well-Argued Prose Rulebook

**57 rules · 9 levels · argument-first scholarly writing**

The governing principle is simple: **a concept must earn its place in an argument.** Precise concepts emerge from actual inferential pressure and continue to perform identifiable work.

**Scope.** English-language philosophy, ethics, value theory, and argument-driven normative scholarship; other disciplines may adapt the standards to their evidence and conventions.

**How to read each rule.** *Standard* defines the requirement; *Corpus observation* describes a transferable writing move from the twelve-paper reference corpus; *Review action* supplies an executable inspection; *Example* illustrates its application. These **source-linked examples are newly written editorial paraphrases**, not verbatim excerpts or the original authors' exact sentences. Corpus observations are interpretive, not official journal instructions.

## 1. Manuscript Architecture

<a id="rule-01"></a>

### Rule 01 — Begin with pressure internal to the object

**Scope:** Title, opening paragraph, abstract, introduction, whole-manuscript review.

**Standard.** Open with an unresolved difficulty arising from the subject itself: a conceptual fracture, conflicting judgment, tacit operation, explanatory failure, normative risk, or recalcitrant case. General importance and disciplinary background can locate the difficulty but cannot generate it.

**Corpus observation.** Foot moves from the continuing appeal of utilitarianism to a problem within consequentialist reasoning; Edgington contrasts fluent conditional practice with explanatory disagreement; Loew derives a question about mortal choice from Borges.

**Review action.** State the internal pressure in one sentence. Verify that the opening 350 words make the difficulty and its stakes legible; replace field-level publicity or a procession of sources with the problem.

**Example.** Weak: “Conditionals have attracted considerable philosophical attention.” Stronger: “Speakers readily distinguish ordinary uses of ‘if’, yet theories of conditional meaning disagree about what makes those uses intelligible.” The second opening moves from familiar linguistic competence to a problem an account must explain.

**Source article(s).** [Dorothy Edgington (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/104.414.235)

<a id="rule-02"></a>

### Rule 02 — Give the manuscript one load-bearing claim

**Scope:** Argument design, title, abstract, section plan, and conclusion.

**Standard.** The load-bearing claim is the defensible proposition a reader can state after setting aside the manuscript’s detail. Literature, concepts, cases, objections, and conclusion must advance that claim without splitting it into unrelated theses.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel focuses the sum of well-being on an additive concatenation condition; Friedman explains inquiry through question-directed intention; Nava characterizes adoption as a diachronic learning process.

**Review action.** Draft one sentence in this form: judgment X requires condition Y; account Z explains Y and is tested against cases H. Assign each section a necessary part of that claim.

**Example.** Central claim, reconstructed from Nebel: treating well-being as additively measurable requires a suitable concatenation operation on its bearers. Proposals for that operation involve substantive assumptions about value. The paper tests those assumptions rather than simply announcing that aggregation is mistaken.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

<a id="rule-03"></a>

### Rule 03 — Convert a topic into a condition question

**Scope:** Research questions, introduction, abstract, contribution statements.

**Standard.** A topic names an area; a condition question identifies what must obtain for an existing judgment, practice, norm, or operation to be intelligible or valid. Prefer questions that yield definable conditions and possible counterexamples.

**Corpus observation.** Baysan reframes the source of normativity as a grounding problem; Nebel examines the operation presupposed by aggregation; Landrum asks what filters competing candidate contents.

**Review action.** Ask: What makes this possible? What operation has been assumed? Under what condition does this judgment hold? Continue until there is a proposition that can be tested and challenged.

**Example.** Weak: “This article concerns how concepts acquire contents.” Stronger: “What selects orang-utans, rather than Sumatran orang-utans or apes, as the content of a concept when all three properties can figure in its causal history?” The latter asks for a discriminating condition.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-04"></a>

### Rule 04 — Write contributions with position, condition, move, and limit

**Scope:** Abstract, final introduction paragraph, cover letter, review response, conclusion.

**Standard.** A contribution statement must locate the debate, expose a condition the debate leaves insufficiently specified, identify the paper’s argumentative move, and state the reach of its result. Self-praise cannot substitute for these four components.

**Corpus observation.** Melamedoff presents an emergentist interpretation as explaining multiple aspects of Shepherd’s causal view; Nebel makes the permissibility of a sum depend on a defensible operation.

**Review action.** Underline the four elements in the proposed contribution. If any is absent, revise before choosing stronger adjectives or claims of novelty.

**Example.** Contribution reconstruction: a proposal to ground fundamental normative facts in the essences of normative properties raises two tests: whether the explanation answers the source question and whether its purported grounds are non-normative. The criticism targets that explanatory strategy; it does not purport to refute normativity itself.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070)

<a id="rule-05"></a>

### Rule 05 — Make the strength of a claim answerable to its constraints

**Scope:** Abstract, major claims, section conclusions, conclusion, reviewer response.

**Standard.** A strong conclusion should exhibit its conditions of application, evidential standing, treatment of rival explanations, and possible failure. Substantive boundaries strengthen an argument when they are entailed by its route of justification.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel questions what may be taken for granted rather than asserting impossibility; Baysan targets a particular explanatory strategy; Hubacher Haerle ties a success norm to rational beliefs about inquiry.

**Review action.** For each major claim record the condition, supporting evidence or reasoning, rival account, and circumstances that would force revision.

**Example.** Weak: “No one can add well-being across persons.” Better calibrated: “The available candidates for concatenating bearers of well-being impose contested commitments; consequently, an additive total requires argument rather than presumption.” This preserves Nebel’s stated refusal to declare those commitments unacceptable.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

<a id="rule-06"></a>

### Rule 06 — Introduce theoretical boundaries early

**Scope:** Abstract, first definition, opening pages, cases, objections.

**Standard.** State the theory’s register, object, non-implications, and points of failure when the central idea first appears. Boundaries developed only after objections can look improvised; boundaries applied throughout the analysis guide judgments.

**Corpus observation.** Friedman delimits individual inquiry early; Baysan states the commitments presupposed by a specific grounding question; Nebel distinguishes questioning a sum from proving that no sum exists.

**Review action.** At first definition, specify object, level, limits, and failure mode; put each boundary to work again in a case or objection.

**Example.** Friedman’s account addresses individual inquiry by appealing to intentions directed at answering questions. Curiosity and wonder remain relevant neighboring attitudes, but the explanatory target is the intentional state and associated activity that constitute an individual inquiry. The stated scope prevents unargued conclusions about every collective research practice.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

## 2. Section Logic

<a id="rule-07"></a>

### Rule 07 — Assign each section one primary argumentative action

**Scope:** Outlining, headings, section opening, structural revision.

**Standard.** A section may contain heterogeneous material, but it must perform one principal operation: reconstruct, define, derive, test, rebut, delimit, or conclude. Material with no relation to that operation belongs elsewhere.

**Corpus observation.** Devitt and Roberts organize their account around acceptance, practice, and skill; Friedman moves from defining QDI to competitors and norms; Melamedoff links textual problems to features of emergence.

**Review action.** For every section complete: “This section establishes X so that the next section can examine Y.” Reassign paragraphs that do not serve X.

**Example.** Illustrative architecture drawn from Friedman’s account: establish what makes an episode an inquiry; specify question-directed intention; compare rival attitudes; explain onset and norms; then derive planning pressures. Each stage performs a separate job in supporting one account of inquiry.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-08"></a>

### Rule 08 — Order sections by increasing argumentative pressure

**Scope:** Outlines, section reordering, submission review.

**Standard.** Sequence the argument as emergence of a difficulty, specification of a condition, development of an explanatory mechanism, testing, boundary-setting, discipline of judgment, and a conclusion. Each section should inherit unfinished work from its predecessor.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington proceeds from classifications of conditionals to theories of truth conditions, probability, validity, and compounds; Nava progresses through a model of adoption, regress worries, and implications.

**Review action.** Annotate each section with the problem still unresolved at the end of the previous one. Move a section if no dependency can be shown.

**Example.** Start with the adoption puzzle: an agent must learn to use a rule without already applying the rule being learned. Distinguish inferential guidance during learning from the competence produced by learning; then address regress and the role of auxiliary rules. The next stage is motivated by a difficulty created at the previous stage.

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-09"></a>

### Rule 09 — Make section openings inherit the preceding result

**Scope:** First paragraph of every section; transitional revision.

**Standard.** The opening of a section should convert an established result into the next necessary question. Reintroducing the whole field at each new heading breaks the continuity of philosophical reasoning.

**Corpus observation.** Loew moves from a case of immortality to probabilistic reasoning and then to its bearing on life quality; Hubacher Haerle moves from inquiry’s aim to a condition on rational success.

**Review action.** Identify the preceding result, its unresolved aspect, and the new task. Integrate them into a natural opening rather than a mechanical retrospective.

**Example.** Section-opening model: interpreting Shepherd’s causes and effects as numerically identical conflicts with her prohibition on self-causation. That conflict gives the next section a definite task: explain their synchronic connection through a relation compatible with distinct cause and effect.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-10"></a>

### Rule 10 — End sections by upgrading the judgment

**Scope:** Section endings, transitions, final synthesis.

**Standard.** A section ending should increase what the reader can now establish, question, or discriminate. A summary that merely repeats the section’s subject contributes no inferential momentum.

**Corpus observation.** Watson’s review repeatedly exposes residual problems about self-determination and possibilities; Nebel tests successive proposals and passes the remaining pressure forward.

**Review action.** Finish by stating what the current result changes and which new constraint now demands attention.

**Example.** Section-ending model: a causal link alone admits too many candidate contents for a concept. Having established that overgeneration, the next question is which explanatory virtues—proportionality, homogeneity, and distal balance—can narrow the candidates without arbitrary selection.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-11"></a>

### Rule 11 — Protect the distinctive job of each manuscript component

**Scope:** Abstract, introduction, literature, theory, cases, objections, conclusion.

**Standard.** The abstract compresses an argument; the introduction creates a question; literature reconstructs a debate; theory specifies conditions; cases expose stress; objections internalize risk; and the conclusion returns a bounded verdict. Mixing these jobs reduces reviewability.

**Corpus observation.** The reviewed Mind corpus shows clear functional separation even where classic and contemporary papers differ greatly in organization.

**Review action.** Name the job of each manuscript component. Rebuild an abstract that reads like a table of contents, a case section that only illustrates, or a conclusion that merely recapitulates.

**Example.** A source-of-normativity introduction should isolate the grounding question; the theoretical analysis should test how essence facts purport to answer it; the objection section should assess explanatory informativeness and normative status. Repeating general claims about the importance of normativity in each component would obscure their distinct functions.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070)

<a id="rule-12"></a>

### Rule 12 — Make literature serve the section’s action

**Scope:** Literature review, theoretical positioning, opening references.

**Standard.** Each cited work must establish a problem, develop a thesis, sharpen a difficulty, supply a resource, create a counterexample, or constrain a conclusion. Sequence the conversation by these functions rather than by surnames or dates alone.

**Corpus observation.** Watson reorganizes free-will positions by explanatory tension; Nebel uses population ethics to expose a common assumption; Baysan isolates a target grounding strategy.

**Review action.** Assign an argumentative verb to each cluster of sources. Combine sources that perform the same role; separate citations with different inferential jobs.

**Example.** Two literature functions: Foot acknowledges the appeal of utilitarianism while bringing the role of virtues into critical focus; Devitt and Roberts confront the adoption problem by examining acceptance and logical practice. In each case, an author enters a literature paragraph to perform an argumentative task—not to increase the bibliography’s length.

**Source article(s).** [Philippa Foot (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XCIV.374.196); [Michael Devitt and Jillian Rose Roberts (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad010)

## 3. Functional Components

<a id="rule-13"></a>

### Rule 13 — Treat the abstract as a compressed argument

**Scope:** Abstract drafting, final abstract review, journal adaptation.

**Standard.** An abstract should identify the problem, the relevant dispute, the unresolved condition, the paper’s move, the working concept, its test, and the reach of the conclusion. It is neither a full bibliography nor an exercise in promotional language.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel makes the route through measurement theory visible in the abstract; Friedman states central commitments of QDI; Baysan makes two objections and their domain explicit.

**Review action.** Check for a problem, debate position, gap, concept or mechanism, test, and boundary. Individual sentences may perform multiple jobs, but none should be ornamental.

**Example.** Compressed-abstract model based on Hubacher Haerle: inquiry is an intentional action; that fact supplies a constraint on rationally initiating it; a success norm connects the constraint to prospects of epistemic improvement; the result permits rational suspension of judgment without further inquiry in some circumstances.

**Source article(s).** [Pablo Hubacher Haerle (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf057)

<a id="rule-14"></a>

### Rule 14 — Resolve the introductory question on the first page

**Scope:** Opening 350–600 words; editorial screening; revision for a new journal.

**Standard.** The first page should establish an identifiable problem, a dispute in which it matters, and the paper’s specific gap. A novel term can appear toward the end of this opening; its full apparatus may be introduced later.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington juxtaposes ordinary conditional competence with theoretical disagreement; Foot starts from utilitarianism’s enduring pull; Loew begins with an imaginative case that yields a mechanism.

**Review action.** By approximately 350 words, identify the problem and debate; by approximately 600, make the argumentative move recognizable. Treat these counts as review prompts, not hard journal requirements.

**Example.** Loew begins with an immortal described by Borges and turns the story into a philosophical contrast: under indefinite repetition, the influence of particular choices on the long-run pattern of a life changes. The literary case introduces an argument about agency and chance rather than substituting for that argument.

**Source article(s).** [Christian Loew (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf041)

<a id="rule-15"></a>

### Rule 15 — Specify criteria before proliferating terminology

**Scope:** Theory sections; first extended definition; conceptual development.

**Standard.** Once a central concept has been introduced, its usefulness depends on criteria for application, characteristic failures, tests, and boundaries with neighboring concepts. Further labels should follow this work rather than substitute for it.

**Corpus observation.** Friedman tests QDI against the start and continuation of inquiry, its norms, and planning rationality; Landrum evaluates candidates through explanatory virtues.

**Review action.** For each proposed condition provide its definition, the impostor it excludes, a test case, and its relation to an adjacent concept.

**Example.** Landrum’s candidate contents supply a clean test. A merely causal criterion admits orang-utans, Sumatran orang-utans, and broader ape properties; proportionality, homogeneity, and distal balance are then evaluated for their ability to exclude unsuitable candidates. The conditions earn their names by ruling something out.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-16"></a>

### Rule 16 — Make cases place the theory under pressure

**Scope:** Hard-case section, controlled comparisons, normative risk analysis.

**Standard.** A case has argumentative force when it targets a condition, threatens a boundary, or reveals a failure. After the case, explain whether the account survives, requires refinement, or must contract.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington uses contrasting conditional cases to test a distinction; Nava’s Harry example exposes an adoption problem; Loew’s immortal cases test the value of global choice.

**Review action.** State what the case tests before presenting it, and identify its consequence for the theory afterwards.

**Example.** A case of repeated inquiry with no rational prospect of epistemic improvement presses the Success Norm: continued inquiry may be epistemically defective even when practical costs are set aside. The critical qualification matters: rational uncertainty about success can still permit inquiry; failure requires stronger negative evidence.

**Source article(s).** [Pablo Hubacher Haerle (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf057)

<a id="rule-17"></a>

### Rule 17 — Organize objections by kinds of pressure

**Scope:** Objections section, referee responses, conceptual defenses.

**Standard.** Treat objections as families of threats to ontology, interpretation, inference, or moral-political application. Each family should expose a genuine point at which the theory could fail rather than become a series of easy replies.

**Corpus observation.** Baysan organizes criticism around informativeness and normative status; Friedman groups challenges that target the explanatory commitments of an intention account.

**Review action.** For each objection category state the strongest version, the endangered premise or conclusion, the necessary distinction, and any warranted concession.

**Example.** Group objections by the premise they threaten. Baysan’s challenge to essence-based grounding separates explanatory adequacy from the normative status of the grounds. Nava’s reply to the adoption regress instead concerns what sort of inferential guidance is available before competence. Treating these as generic “possible criticisms” would lose the relevant tests.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070); [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-18"></a>

### Rule 18 — Write the conclusion as a bounded verdict

**Scope:** Final paragraphs, final abstract sentence, cover-letter contribution.

**Standard.** A conclusion should state what condition has been established, how the original question has changed, and which related questions remain distinct. Its scope must follow the argument actually made.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel concludes with a challenge to assuming aggregation; Baysan targets one account of essence facts; Loew qualifies a benefit of mortality through the role of chance and choice.

**Review action.** Identify the established condition, the transformed problem, and the boundaries that remain in force; remove claims about solving an entire discipline unless actually shown.

**Example.** Bounded conclusion: Nebel’s examination of concatenation candidates leaves the permissibility of their commitments open while challenging an assumed sum of well-being. The argument supports a demand for justification; it supplies no categorical theorem that every possible aggregate is incoherent.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

## 4. Paragraph Argument

<a id="rule-19"></a>

### Rule 19 — Give every paragraph a single primary action

**Scope:** Every body paragraph; paragraph merging and splitting.

**Standard.** A paragraph should principally pose a difficulty, define, distinguish, explain, supply evidence, test, answer, limit, or transition. It can contain several subordinate moves provided their common function is identifiable.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington frequently builds a paragraph around a classificatory stress; Nebel around one measurement condition; Landrum around one explanatory virtue.

**Review action.** Label the paragraph with an action verb. Revise if it lacks an action; split incompatible principal actions; consider joining consecutive short paragraphs that merely repeat one.

**Example.** Weak paragraph plan: report Shepherd’s historical dates, quote her causal claims, define emergence, and address scientific background in one passage. Stronger plan: isolate the incompatibility between literal cause–effect identity and her causal principle, establishing the exact question an emergentist reading must solve.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-20"></a>

### Rule 20 — Use opening sentences for direction and endings for advance

**Scope:** Paragraph revision, transitions, elimination of disconnected fragments.

**Standard.** An opening sentence should identify the relevant relation or question; a closing sentence should change the reader’s grasp of the issue. Simple restatement at the end confuses completion with progress.

**Corpus observation.** Nava often moves from a worry to the consequences of his adoption model; Baysan moves from clarification to the limitations of a particular strategy.

**Review action.** Read each paragraph’s first and last sentences in isolation. Replace generic entrances and repetitive endings with a visible directional movement.

**Example.** Paragraph trajectory: begin with the distinction between following an explicit rule statement and already possessing competence to apply the rule. Then explain why the former can guide the adoption process while the latter is its outcome. End by showing how this separation blocks an immediate regress.

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-21"></a>

### Rule 21 — Reserve very short paragraphs for genuine pivots

**Scope:** Paragraph-length review, introduction and conclusion revision.

**Standard.** A short paragraph can carry a decisive thesis, a principle, or a strong pivot. An ordinary explanatory sentence stranded on its own makes the manuscript read like an outline.

**Corpus observation.** Foot’s brief judgments are supported by prior argumentative pressure; the shorter paragraphs in contemporary Mind papers often mark principles, definitions, or a roadmap.

**Review action.** Review paragraphs under roughly 60 words. Integrate those that merely announce a function; retain those whose separation creates genuine inferential emphasis.

**Example.** A short verdict such as “Mortality changes what choices can settle” needs the surrounding probabilistic explanation: finite agents can influence an overall distribution of actions, whereas limitless repetition can wash out the contribution of a particular choice. Alone, the verdict would be an unsupported slogan.

**Source article(s).** [Christian Loew (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf041)

<a id="rule-22"></a>

### Rule 22 — Organize literature paragraphs by argumentative function

**Scope:** Literature review paragraphs, critical introduction, theory positioning.

**Standard.** Group works that collectively construct a problem, supply a premise, deepen a counterexample, or open a further question. Chronological author-by-author narration should not replace reconstruction of the disagreement.

**Corpus observation.** Watson revisits compatibilism, hierarchical accounts, and libertarian worries as parts of a problem; Nebel uses population ethics to display an assumption about total well-being.

**Review action.** Write the paragraph’s function first, then order authors by their contribution to that function. End with the exact question the new work inherits.

**Example.** Functional grouping example: Watson organizes competing questions about freedom around an agent’s control and available alternatives. Devitt and Roberts investigate whether acceptance of a logical principle suffices for change in inferential practice. In each paragraph, sources should be arranged by the argumentative issue they establish—not by publication date.

**Source article(s).** [Gary Watson (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XCVI.382.145); [Michael Devitt and Jillian Rose Roberts (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad010)

<a id="rule-23"></a>

### Rule 23 — Give case paragraphs an explicit test function

**Scope:** Thought experiments, comparisons, hard-case paragraphs.

**Standard.** The reader should understand which property or distinction the case is designed to test before interpreting its details. Conclude by identifying what the result changes in the account.

**Corpus observation.** Nava introduces Harry after the adoption problem has been established; Edgington’s paired cases bear on conditional dualism; Loew’s immortality cases test the weight of global choices.

**Review action.** Privately label each case as testing a condition, exposing a counterfeit, offering a counterexample, or delimiting an implication; ensure the prose performs that role even if the label disappears.

**Example.** Opening function: test whether causal relevance is enough to fix the content of an inductive concept. The orang-utan case then contrasts narrower and broader candidate properties. Closing function: the multiplicity of causal explanations requires a further selection principle; the example thus changes the theoretical task.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-24"></a>

### Rule 24 — Make transitions convert results into new questions

**Scope:** Section links, theory-to-case moves, objections, pre-conclusion.

**Standard.** A successful transition uses a result already defended to generate the next necessary question. Independent summaries joined by a heading are not an argument.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington’s extended account progresses through the limitations of each treatment; Friedman moves from a competing attitude-based account to QDI; Hubacher Haerle derives normative pressure from the aim of inquiry.

**Review action.** State what is settled, what remains unresolved, and why the next section is the appropriate place to address it.

**Example.** Transition model: identifying question-directed intentions explains how an agent undertakes a particular inquiry. The result creates a new question: how do rational constraints on intending explain apparent norms about ignorance and the choice of means? The second issue emerges from the first.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-25"></a>

### Rule 25 — Require each paragraph to survive the deletion test

**Scope:** Compression, anti-repetition review, final revision.

**Standard.** A paragraph earns its place by supplying a necessary condition, distinction, argument, evidence, counterexample, boundary, or transition. If removing it only reduces atmosphere, it is not yet doing enough work.

**Corpus observation.** Removing Nebel’s measurement conditions, Friedman’s QDI claims, or Nava’s guidance/use distinction would damage the corresponding inference.

**Review action.** Delete the paragraph temporarily and identify the missing inferential contribution. Preserve and strengthen indispensable paragraphs; merge or cut redundant roadmaps.

**Example.** Remove a paragraph that merely repeats “the source of normativity is important” and the criticism remains intact. Remove the distinction between an informative grounding explanation and the normative status of proposed grounds, and Baysan’s two main objections collapse into one. The latter paragraph has indispensable argumentative work.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070)

## 5. Conceptual Engineering

<a id="rule-26"></a>

### Rule 26 — Let conceptual names emerge from explanatory pressure

**Scope:** New theoretical vocabulary, naming, titles, first mention.

**Standard.** Introduce a new term once the preceding analysis has shown a relation or operation that existing descriptions fail to capture adequately. A good concept gives precise form to a demonstrated need.

**Corpus observation.** Landrum’s Filtering Problem names pressure created by competing causal candidates; Friedman’s QDI answers weaknesses in accounts based on inquisitive attitudes. The source also invokes the historical naming of the prion as a separate illustration.

**Review action.** Before naming, identify what established accounts explain, the point where they leave a gap, and the relation the new term denotes.

**Example.** Landrum’s Filtering Problem arises because several properties can explain why a concept is used or acquired without all being its contents. Once the failure of unfiltered causal explanation is visible, the name “Filtering Problem” identifies a determinate theoretical task rather than adding decorative terminology.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-27"></a>

### Rule 27 — Define concepts by object, relation, limit, result, and failure

**Scope:** First definition, formal propositions, core theoretical paragraphs.

**Standard.** A first definition should fix the object of inquiry, the relation at issue, the level at which it operates, its adjacent boundaries, the consequence of its application, and the characteristic counter-structure when it fails.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel connects concatenation to additive representation; Nava defines adoption as a process yielding competence; Baysan specifies what demands metaphysical explanation.

**Review action.** Follow the definition promptly with a boundary and failure case. Thereafter apply the definition rather than repeatedly redefining the term.

**Example.** Definition reconstruction: a question-directed intention is an intention to answer some question; its target is individual inquiry, and beginning to act in its service marks the onset of inquiry on the proposed account. Curiosity without that practical commitment illustrates a neighboring state that the definition does not treat as sufficient.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-28"></a>

### Rule 28 — Make the work of each concept visible

**Scope:** Core theoretical argument, introduction of new notions, contribution review.

**Standard.** A theoretical term must do identifiable work: explain a gap, establish a boundary, exclude a proxy, distinguish cases, or support a conclusion. Repetition of the word without added explanatory function generates conceptual density rather than insight.

**Corpus observation.** QDI connects inquiry identity, initiation, norms, and instrumental pressure; concatenation conditions clarify what addition presupposes; an emergentist reading unifies features of Shepherd’s account.

**Review action.** List at least three distinct tasks for the central concept and trace each to paragraphs where the manuscript performs it.

**Example.** The notion of a question-directed intention must explain more than a psychological label: it identifies inquiry, helps locate its beginning, recasts ignorance-related norms, and connects inquiry to rational requirements on planning. A draft should show each explanatory task where the theory is used.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-29"></a>

### Rule 29 — Preserve the legitimate jurisdiction of neighboring concepts

**Scope:** Concept comparison, rival theories, normative boundaries.

**Standard.** An argument gains precision by specifying what adjacent ideas explain well, then identifying the different function of the new account. Describing every alternative as mistaken produces false novelty and conceptual overreach.

**Corpus observation.** Watson keeps autonomy and possibilities in view; Nebel leaves open theories that can defend an additive structure; Baysan restricts the assessment to views that share relevant presuppositions.

**Review action.** Describe each concept by its object and function before asserting a difference. Preserve its successes when stating the new account’s contribution.

**Example.** Acceptance of a statement of universal instantiation, habitual inferential skill, and adoption of the rule concern related but distinguishable achievements. Devitt and Roberts analyze the tension between acceptance and practice; Nava explains how instruction might guide the transition. Retain each notion’s legitimate role instead of treating them as interchangeable.

**Source article(s).** [Michael Devitt and Jillian Rose Roberts (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad010); [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-30"></a>

### Rule 30 — Use conditions to exclude specific counterfeits

**Scope:** Constitutive conditions, theories with multiple criteria, hard cases.

**Standard.** A condition is more than a descriptive category when it prevents a recognisable false substitute from counting as the target phenomenon. Every necessary condition should have a motivating failure mode.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington tests the boundary between kinds of conditional; Landrum filters problematic candidates using explanatory virtues; Nava’s model rules out a regress-producing account of rule adoption.

**Review action.** Match every theoretical condition with at least one false positive it would exclude. Reconsider criteria with no corresponding failure risk.

**Example.** A condition has argumentative force when it eliminates a live mistake. Distal balance, proportionality, and homogeneity are proposed to distinguish explanatorily appropriate contents from competing causal properties in Landrum’s account. Simply listing these virtues without showing which candidate each excludes would not resolve the Filtering Problem.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-31"></a>

### Rule 31 — Make boundaries operative throughout the theory

**Scope:** Definitions, hard cases, objections, conclusion.

**Standard.** A boundary should regulate which judgments are licensed, which inferences are barred, and where evidence is insufficient. A disclaimer that never changes a theoretical result has not yet been integrated.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel repeatedly tests the commitments of rival accounts rather than appending caution only at the end; Friedman maintains a distinction between QDI and neighboring attitudes.

**Review action.** Track each main limit from its first statement into at least one case, objection, and final judgment.

**Example.** The Success Norm applies to inquiry as intentional action and permits inquiry when belief in success—or suspension about success—is rational. That qualification must guide the cases and final claim: uncertainty is not automatically a reason to stop; inquiry becomes prohibited when neither attitude is rationally available.

**Source article(s).** [Pablo Hubacher Haerle (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf057)

<a id="rule-32"></a>

### Rule 32 — Control technical-term density by conceptual work

**Scope:** Whole-manuscript polish, repeated technical terms, prose naturalness.

**Standard.** Repeat a technical expression when it supplies a stable reference for an active argument. Replace redundant recurrence with clearly identified objects, relations, conditions, or consequences when no new work is performed.

**Corpus observation.** Mind papers may repeat technical expressions such as QDI, concatenation, or Filtering Problem, but their repeated use typically tracks explanatory tasks.

**Review action.** At each recurrence ask whether the term defines, constrains, tests, rejects, or concludes something. Reduce appearances that perform none of these jobs.

**Example.** After defining adoption, vary explanatory phrasing without shifting the technical distinction: an explicit rule statement guides a learning process; the ability to apply that rule is produced through the process; its later exercise is rule use. Repeating “adoption” alone would conceal these different stages.

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

## 6. Inference and Testing

<a id="rule-33"></a>

### Rule 33 — Use controlled comparisons to isolate a key variable

**Scope:** Thought experiments, ethical comparisons, value theory, causal/explanatory tests.

**Standard.** Hold salient familiar factors constant while varying the relation whose importance is disputed. A controlled comparison has force when the resulting difference can be traced to the proposed explanatory variable rather than an unreported change.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel contrasts measurements with and without a meaningful addition operation; Loew tests mortality against kinds of immortality; life-shape cases often hold total goods constant while altering temporal order.

**Review action.** Specify Case A, Case B, common properties, the single intended difference, and the conclusion the contrast licenses; examine confounders.

**Example.** Controlled contrast inspired by Loew: consider a mortal and an indefinitely surviving agent who face otherwise similar local decisions. Hold the kinds of action and their immediate outcomes fixed; vary whether the horizon is finite. Ask how far present choices can determine the overall pattern when the number of future choices is unbounded.

**Source article(s).** [Christian Loew (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf041)

<a id="rule-34"></a>

### Rule 34 — Derive distinctions from a case of failure

**Scope:** Concept differentiation, theoretical reconstruction, objections.

**Standard.** A distinction earns its place when an ambiguity produces an error, a counterexample, or a breakdown in explanation. State the failed inference before introducing a pair of technical labels.

**Corpus observation.** Nava separates learning to adopt a rule from later using it under regress pressure; Baysan scrutinizes what makes a grounding answer informative; Edgington uses divergent conditional cases.

**Review action.** Show what goes wrong without the distinction and which precise inference becomes possible after it is made.

**Example.** The distinction between numerical identity and synchronic dependence is forced by a problem in Shepherd’s texts: she describes a cause and its effect as simultaneous yet forbids a being to cause itself. An emergentist reading preserves dependence without treating the two relata as numerically identical.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-35"></a>

### Rule 35 — Convert serious objections into constraints on the model

**Scope:** Objections, theory development, reviewer engagement.

**Standard.** Where a powerful objection exposes a genuine risk, incorporate its lesson as a condition, parameter, evidential demand, or boundary. The point of response is to improve explanatory architecture, not to win a sequence of isolated exchanges.

**Corpus observation.** Nava treats the regress challenge as a requirement on an adoption model; Nebel turns an additive assumption into a measurement condition; Baysan formulates explanatory tests for an essence-based strategy.

**Review action.** Translate the strongest objection into: “If this challenge is sound, the account must satisfy X.” Then demonstrate whether and how the revised conditions satisfy X.

**Example.** The regress objection becomes a design constraint: if adoption requires already using the rule being adopted, learning never begins. Nava’s model therefore distinguishes being guided by a statement of the rule from exercising the new competence, and specifies auxiliary rules that can operate during learning.

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-36"></a>

### Rule 36 — Establish explanatory superiority rather than announce it

**Scope:** Interpretive philosophy, competing accounts, cross-field conceptual transfers.

**Standard.** An interpretation gains force by explaining several features together with fewer ad hoc assumptions than rivals. Identifying the rival’s partial success and residual failures is more persuasive than claiming a novel reading is obviously correct.

**Corpus observation.** Melamedoff uses emergence to interpret diverse features of Shepherd’s metaphysics; Landrum adapts virtues of causal explanation to a metasemantic filtering problem.

**Review action.** List the target phenomena, show how the account explains their connections, identify the rival explanation, and locate what it leaves unresolved.

**Example.** An emergentist interpretation is assessed by how well it jointly explains Shepherd’s causal mixture, novel effects, synchronic dependence, and historical scientific context. A rival literal-identity reading faces tension with her causal principle. Explanatory reach, rather than the mere assertion of a preferred label, carries the case.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-37"></a>

### Rule 37 — Supply inferential bridges before strong connectives

**Scope:** Sentence revision, paragraph argument, section transitions, conclusion.

**Standard.** “Therefore,” “thus,” and “hence” cannot supply missing premises. A valid transition needs an identifiable object, condition, mediating relation, and implication; conspicuous connectives without those elements mimic argumentative progress.

**Corpus observation.** Measurement conditions, grounding conditions, adoption processes, success norms, and answerability conditions all exemplify explicit mediating steps in the corpus.

**Review action.** For each strong connective write the intermediate premise that licenses it. If none can be supplied, change the claim or remove the connective.

**Example.** Inferential bridge: inquiries are intentional actions; the rationality of such actions depends on their prospect of success; inquiry success is understood in terms of epistemic improvement. Only with those steps can a restriction on intentional action support an epistemic norm of inquiry.

**Source article(s).** [Pablo Hubacher Haerle (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf057)

<a id="rule-38"></a>

### Rule 38 — Derive the limit of a conclusion from its proof

**Scope:** Major claims, abstract, section verdicts, conclusion.

**Standard.** The modal and evidential strength of a conclusion must match the route by which it was established. Distinguish a proof of necessity from plausibility, explanatory superiority, diagnostic pressure, and the defeat of a presupposition.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel’s scrutiny of proposed concatenation operations licenses caution toward a presupposed sum; Hubacher Haerle derives a norm with a defined scope from the character of intentional inquiry.

**Review action.** Mark the result as existence, necessity, conceptual pressure, explanatory advantage, or defeasible plausibility. Revise any wording that outruns the established category.

**Example.** From failures to identify an uncontroversial concatenation operation, the warranted conclusion is that well-being totals should not be assumed without argument. The reasoning need not deny every candidate operation or prove that all additive representations are impossible.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

## 7. Sentence Logic

<a id="rule-39"></a>

### Rule 39 — Make sentences express relations

**Scope:** English revision, abstracts, definitions, core claims, conclusions.

**Standard.** A scholarly sentence should ordinarily show how object A explains, changes, supports, restricts, or depends upon object B under a condition. Abstract praise or decoration should not displace the proposition.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel’s prose turns on the relationship between measurement and addition; Friedman between QDI and planning rationality; Landrum between explanatory virtues and content filtering.

**Review action.** Identify the subject, structural verb, target object, and condition or result. Restore any missing relation before polishing cadence.

**Example.** Weak: “The Filtering Problem is a deeply complex challenge.” Stronger: “Several causally relevant properties compete to determine a concept’s content; explanatory virtues must discriminate among them.” The second sentence supplies an object, a relation, and a test.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-40"></a>

### Rule 40 — Let short sentences judge and long sentences carry structure

**Scope:** Sentence rhythm, definitions, propositions, explanation.

**Standard.** Short sentences can carry a definition, finding, or pivot; longer ones can coordinate conditions, scope, and causal explanation. Uniform sentence length weakens hierarchy even when each sentence is grammatical.

**Corpus observation.** Foot uses compact ethical judgments, while Edgington can sustain longer sentences involving classificatory or logical relations; recent Mind abstracts often couple claims with their conditions.

**Review action.** Keep the sentences that express decisive judgments crisp; give the surrounding explanation enough syntactic room to establish why they hold.

**Example.** Short verdict: “The horizon changes the significance of choice.” The longer explanation must distinguish finite sequences, whose overall pattern can be influenced by decisions, from infinite sequences in which relative frequencies may converge toward underlying probabilities. Sentence length tracks argumentative complexity.

**Source article(s).** [Christian Loew (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf041)

<a id="rule-41"></a>

### Rule 41 — Prefer positive relations to formulaic negative pivots

**Scope:** Sentence polish, conceptual distinctions, anti-template review.

**Standard.** A “not X but Y” structure is justified when the contrast itself does genuine philosophical work. When it merely adds force, state the different functions of X and Y and their relation directly.

**Corpus observation.** Watson’s contrasts can reorganize a problem; superficial oppositions elsewhere risk mistaking rhetorical intensity for a genuine theoretical distinction.

**Review action.** For each negative pivot, ask what false inference it blocks. If there is no such inference, rewrite it as a positive relationship.

**Example.** Weak: “Shepherd’s causation is not ordinary causation but emergence.” Stronger: “On the emergentist interpretation, effects depend synchronically on mixtures of constituents and exhibit qualities that those constituents separately lack.” The positive relation clarifies the claim without a theatrical opposition.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-42"></a>

### Rule 42 — Use first person to assume argumentative responsibility

**Scope:** Abstract, introduction, roadmaps, conclusions, response letters.

**Standard.** “I argue,” “I show,” and “I suggest” are legitimate when the author is committing to a distinct inference or calibrating its strength. First person adds little when it merely announces an intention to discuss.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel uses first person to define what is not being claimed; Baysan marks a specific argumentative move; Friedman states the commitments of the account being defended.

**Review action.** Keep a first-person expression when the following clause names an actual claim or operation. Otherwise let the phenomenon and inference take the grammatical lead.

**Example.** Strong first-person commitment, reconstructed: “I argue that explanations citing property essences do not adequately answer the proposed grounding question and may fail the non-normativity requirement.” Weak: “In this paper I wish to offer an extensive discussion of the source of normativity.” These are editorial model sentences, not quotations.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070)

<a id="rule-43"></a>

### Rule 43 — Write roadmaps as inferential routes

**Scope:** End of introduction, navigation of long papers, section previews.

**Standard.** A roadmap should make clear why each section is needed for the result, not merely report its topic. Use verbs corresponding to operations the paper actually performs.

**Corpus observation.** Melamedoff previews the cumulative support for an interpretation; Loew progresses from Borges to probability, value consequences, and further conceptions of immortality.

**Review action.** Prefer “identifies,” “tests,” “distinguishes,” “shows,” “defends,” and “limits” where warranted; reduce a string of generic “discusses.”

**Example.** Inferential roadmap based on Nava: formulate the adoption problem; separate rule-guided learning from rule use; explain how the process avoids regress; examine the privileged rules that make learning possible; draw out the implication that familiar rules need not be unadoptable. Each verb names a necessary argumentative step.

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-44"></a>

### Rule 44 — Turn overloaded nominal clusters back into verb relations

**Scope:** Abstracts, conceptual definitions, dense theory paragraphs.

**Standard.** Three or more consecutive abstractions can conceal which concept operates on which. Technical English may be dense, but the predicative and logical relations must remain recoverable.

**Corpus observation.** The corpus uses bounded technical expressions such as “question-directed intention,” “natural concatenation operation,” and “Success Norm for Inquiry,” whose jobs can be identified.

**Review action.** Flag extended strings of technical nouns or modifiers, split them where needed, and restore an explicit predicate and target.

**Example.** Weak nominal cluster: “question-directed-intention-based individual-inquiry rationality explanation.” Stronger: “A question-directed intention identifies a particular inquiry. Constraints on forming and carrying out that intention explain the practical pressures on the inquirer.” The second formulation restores agents, verbs, and relations.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-45"></a>

### Rule 45 — Make logical connectives track real logical relations

**Scope:** Inference sentences, paragraph transitions, conclusions.

**Standard.** “Because” signals an explanatory ground, “therefore” an inference, “however” a genuine opposing consideration, and “while” coexistence or contrast. Conjunctions do not make relations true simply by naming them.

**Corpus observation.** Baysan’s explanation of grounding uses conditional and dependence language precisely; Nebel’s measurement discussion makes the antecedent of inferential conditionals explicit.

**Review action.** Inspect every strong connective. If the relation is merely temporal, say so or omit the connective; if a conclusion is asserted, provide its warrant.

**Example.** Misleading: “Well-being is measurable, therefore it is additive.” Correct bridge: additive representation requires a suitable concatenation operation satisfying the axioms of extensive measurement. Without that premise, the connective converts a measurement claim into a stronger conclusion without warrant.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

## 8. Diction and Precision

<a id="rule-46"></a>

### Rule 46 — Prefer verbs that disclose argumentative structure

**Scope:** Editing across abstracts, theory, literature, conclusions.

**Standard.** Verbs such as “presupposes,” “distinguishes,” “explains,” “grounds,” “requires,” “limits,” and “rules out” show what theoretical work is being done. Grand adjectives make weak substitutes for these relations.

**Corpus observation.** The corpus favors identifiable verbs of dependence, exclusion, explanation, qualification, and consequence.

**Review action.** Replace empty evaluations (“important,” “complex,” “deep,” “rich”) where possible with a sentence specifying the relevant relation.

**Example.** Useful verbs from Landrum’s explanatory sequence include “identifies” candidate properties, “filters” rival contents, “compares” causal explanations, and “excludes” unsuitable candidates. Replace “a sophisticated and highly innovative account” with the work those verbs actually describe.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-47"></a>

### Rule 47 — Keep terms stable and their levels distinct

**Scope:** Whole-manuscript terminology audits; conceptual consistency.

**Standard.** Once defined, an indispensable term needs a stable reference. Do not slide among object, condition, criterion, evidence, proxy, boundary, and judgment as though they occupied the same theoretical level.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel keeps sum, concatenation, and extensive measurement apart; Friedman distinguishes QDI, interrogative attitudes, and plan-rationality; Nava separates adoption, guidance, and use.

**Review action.** Build a glossary recording the definition, level, allowable variants, and prohibited conflations of every central term.

**Example.** Terminology ledger: question = the target of an inquiry; question-directed intention = the relevant intentional state; acting in its service = proposed onset condition; plan-rationality = a source of instrumental constraints. Substituting “curiosity” for the defined intention would change the claim.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)

<a id="rule-48"></a>

### Rule 48 — Calibrate modal and evidential language

**Scope:** Theory claims, conclusions, abstracts, review responses.

**Standard.** “May,” “can,” “must,” “requires,” “suggests,” “shows,” “entails,” and “rules out” differ in inferential force. An unwarranted modal overstates a result; excessive softening can hide an established inference.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel uses language suitable to the limits of an argument; Baysan calibrates criticism to an explanatory strategy; Hubacher Haerle specifies the scope of epistemic requirements.

**Review action.** Mark each major modal with the proof level it signals. Use “requires” for established necessity, “explains better” for comparative advantage, and “suggests” where the result is defeasible.

**Example.** The Success Norm permits inquiry under rational suspension about likely success, yet prohibits it when both rational belief in success and rational suspension are unavailable. “Always,” “might,” and “must” cannot be substituted for these conditional modal relations without changing the principle.

**Source article(s).** [Pablo Hubacher Haerle (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf057)

<a id="rule-49"></a>

### Rule 49 — Do not substitute grand adjectives for mechanisms

**Scope:** Titles, abstracts, introductions, conclusions, cover letters.

**Standard.** Self-ascriptions such as “groundbreaking,” “profound,” and “comprehensive” are rhetorically weak without the result they purport to praise. A claim to importance should arise from what the theory establishes.

**Corpus observation.** The observed Mind cases put conceptual revisions and inferential results ahead of promotional self-evaluation.

**Review action.** Search for superlatives and high-praise adjectives; replace each unsupported evaluation with a relation, condition, or discriminating consequence.

**Example.** Weak: “This article offers an extraordinarily profound interpretation of Shepherd.” Stronger: “The emergentist reading explains why Shepherd treats causation as synchronic, why effects depend on mixtures, and why new qualities arise from their constituents.” Explanatory results replace the author’s self-assessment.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-50"></a>

### Rule 50 — Give cited authors a visible argumentative function

**Scope:** Literature reviews, theoretical positioning, opening pages, notes.

**Standard.** An author’s name should introduce a move that establishes, revises, sharpens, challenges, supplies, or limits something. Mere citation placement is no substitute for explaining the source’s role.

**Corpus observation.** Watson and Edgington arrange research by disputes and problems rather than producing a sequence of isolated author summaries.

**Review action.** For each key cited author complete the sentence “This source does X for the argument.” Remove or relocate citations without an identifiable function.

**Example.** Source-function model: “Melamedoff develops an interpretation that accounts for Shepherd’s unusual combination of synchronicity, mixture, and novelty.” In a distinct debate, “Watson reorganizes questions about free action through self-determination and possible alternatives.” The citations carry specified functions instead of ornamental authority.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074); [Gary Watson (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XCVI.382.145)

<a id="rule-51"></a>

### Rule 51 — Do not turn differences of level into rankings of value

**Scope:** Concept comparison, normative distinctions, rival positions.

**Standard.** A difference between explanatory level, evidential role, criterion, authority, or domain does not by itself make one concept “deeper,” “higher,” or “better” than another. Specify jurisdiction before drawing any evaluative conclusion.

**Corpus observation.** Watson separates different features of freedom; Baysan distinguishes explanatory targets; Nebel separates representation from the property being represented.

**Review action.** Rewrite “X is deeper than Y” as two function statements and an explicit description of how they interact.

**Example.** Weak: “Mortal lives are better than immortal lives in every respect.” Stronger: “Loew’s argument identifies one benefit of mortality: finite agents can exercise a kind of control over the global pattern of their choices that becomes less significant over an unbounded horizon.” The revised claim states a respect of difference, not an overall ranking.

**Source article(s).** [Christian Loew (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf041)

## 9. Pre-Submission Review

<a id="rule-52"></a>

### Rule 52 — Review the manuscript-level claim before any line edit

**Scope:** Final polish, resubmission, adaptation to a target journal.

**Standard.** When the central claim drifts, sentence-level improvement only makes an incoherent argument smoother. Verify the result at manuscript level before investing in local elegance.

**Corpus observation.** The twelve-paper corpus illustrates varied forms of a focused central operation: revising a debate, testing aggregation, accounting for inquiry, interpreting Shepherd, or explaining mortality.

**Review action.** Express the contribution in no more than about 50 words. Check that title, abstract, introduction, and conclusion commit to the same defensible thesis.

**Example.** Fifty-word claim reconstruction: adding well-being across persons presupposes a concatenation operation satisfying measurement-theoretic conditions. Candidate operations—within lives, among lives, or among evaluatively basic propositions—carry controversial assumptions. The article makes the aggregation thesis answerable to those assumptions before any sentence-level polish.

**Source article(s).** [Jacob M. Nebel (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzad019)

<a id="rule-53"></a>

### Rule 53 — Review each section for its structural job

**Scope:** Reorganization, merging sections, cutting before submission.

**Standard.** A section must contribute a named argumentative act concerning a specific object and provide the next step with a condition or problem. A section that can only be described as “discussing related issues” remains underdesigned.

**Corpus observation.** Nava, Friedman, and Nebel present extended arguments whose successive sections can be stated in functional terms.

**Review action.** Write a temporary one-sentence specification for each section; remove it after using it to check every paragraph and transition.

**Example.** Section-function sequence in Melamedoff’s account: resolve the apparent identity problem; define the relevant sense of emergence; test whether Shepherd’s metaphysics displays emergentist features; place the reading in its scientific history; distinguish it from Mill’s later tradition. A section titled merely “Background” would conceal these jobs.

**Source article(s).** [Ariel Melamedoff (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae074)

<a id="rule-54"></a>

### Rule 54 — Review every paragraph’s main move

**Scope:** Final structural pass, prose naturalness, paragraph boundaries.

**Standard.** Mark paragraphs as defining, locating, distinguishing, testing, replying, limiting, transitioning, or concluding. Redundant or incompatible action patterns reveal weak architecture even in fluent prose.

**Corpus observation.** Edgington’s longer discussions remain followable through locally identifiable classificatory and inferential steps.

**Review action.** Label every paragraph privately; merge those with duplicated functions, split conflicting jobs, and remove the labels before publication.

**Example.** Three paragraphs repeating that essential properties matter are structurally redundant. Assign separate jobs instead: reconstruct the essence-based explanation, test whether it informs the source-of-normativity question, and test whether the proposed grounds are genuinely non-normative.

**Source article(s).** [Umut Baysan (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae070)

<a id="rule-55"></a>

### Rule 55 — Audit inferential bridges explicitly

**Scope:** Core arguments, connective-heavy passages, transitions.

**Standard.** Where the paper moves from proposition A to conclusion C, it must provide the premise or condition B that licenses the move. An emphatic connective is not a substitute for B.

**Corpus observation.** Nebel’s measurement axioms, Baysan’s grounding requirements, and Nava’s distinction between guidance and use demonstrate the role of mediating structure.

**Review action.** Highlight “therefore,” “thus,” “hence,” and “accordingly”; write the warrant beside each occurrence and repair transitions that lack one.

**Example.** Bridge sequence: stated inference rule → guidance during a learning process → newly acquired ability to use the rule. If the middle step is suppressed, the theory appears to require the very competence whose acquisition it is supposed to explain. The bridge is an actual process distinction, not “therefore.”

**Source article(s).** [William Nava (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf017)

<a id="rule-56"></a>

### Rule 56 — Audit prose for argumentative load

**Scope:** Final English polish and patterned-prose review.

**Standard.** The standard is clarity of relation, stable terminology, durable syntax, and precise conclusions. Formality and rhetorical grandeur are secondary to making the inference recoverable.

**Corpus observation.** The reference corpus shows the value of clean conceptual relations and proportionate claims without requiring a uniformly ornate vocabulary.

**Review action.** Locate adjective clusters, stacked nominalizations, formulaic “not X but Y,” source parades, empty contribution statements, and repeated definitions; revise each against the underlying argumentative relation.

**Example.** Weak: “This study offers a comprehensive and nuanced metasemantic framework.” Stronger: “The account uses proportionality, homogeneity, and distal balance to discriminate among causally relevant candidates for a concept’s content.” The revision names the decisive operation and its target.

**Source article(s).** [Kyle Landrum (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzaf024)

<a id="rule-57"></a>

### Rule 57 — Apply a transparent, human-scored acceptance checklist

**Scope:** Pre-submission review, collaborative editing, version comparison.

**Standard.** Score twelve manuscript-level checks from 0 (absent) to 1 (present but weak) to 2 (clear and operative). A total below 20/24 triggers further argument and structure revision before final language polishing; the score is an internal review aid, not a journal acceptance predictor.

**Corpus observation.** The reviewed papers generally make it possible to identify their main problem, conceptual work, case pressure, objections, and bounded result.

**Review action.** Complete the twelve-item scorecard with textual evidence and revision notes. Review terminology stability separately as a required cross-cutting check under Rule 47.

**Example.** Human-score illustration: inspect whether the manuscript states the problem of individuating inquiry, defines question-directed intention, demonstrates its work on inquiry onset and rational norms, compares competitor attitudes, and limits the conclusion to the defended target. A lexical checker cannot assign the twelve ratings or certify the argument.

**Source article(s).** [Daniel C. Friedman (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzae056)
